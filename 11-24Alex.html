Powers of 10 and exponents.

a.Represent

Write \(456\) in expanded notation using powers of \(10\).

Each digit's value depends on its place. The \(4\) is in the hundreds place, the \(5\) in the tens place, and the \(6\) in the ones place:

\[456 = 4 \times 100 + 5 \times 10 + 6 \times 1\]

Write each place value as a power of \(10\):

\[\boxed{456 = 4 \times 10^{2} + 5 \times 10^{1} + 6 \times 10^{0}}\]

b.

Simplify: \(24.25 \times 10^{3}\)

Multiplying by \(10^{3}\) moves the decimal point \(3\) places to the right:

\[24.25 \;\longrightarrow\; 24{,}250\]

\[\boxed{24{,}250}\]

c.

Simplify: \(25 \times 10^{6}\)

Multiplying by \(10^{6}\) moves the decimal point \(6\) places to the right, adding zeros:

\[25 \;\longrightarrow\; 25{,}000{,}000\]

\[\boxed{25{,}000{,}000}\]

d.

Simplify: \(12.5 \div 10^{3}\)

Dividing by \(10^{3}\) moves the decimal point \(3\) places to the left:

\[12.5 \;\longrightarrow\; 0.0125\]

\[\boxed{0.0125}\]

e.

Simplify: \(4.8 \div 10^{4}\)

Dividing by \(10^{4}\) moves the decimal point \(4\) places to the left:

\[4.8 \;\longrightarrow\; 0.00048\]

\[\boxed{0.00048}\]

f.Generalize

Find the missing exponent: \(10^{3} \cdot 10^{4} = 10^{\square}\)

When multiplying powers with the same base, add the exponents:

\[10^{3} \cdot 10^{4} = 10^{3+4} = 10^{7}\]

\[\boxed{7}\]

g.Generalize

Find the missing exponent: \(10^{8} \div 10^{2} = 10^{\square}\)

When dividing powers with the same base, subtract the exponents:

\[10^{8} \div 10^{2} = 10^{8-2} = 10^{6}\]

\[\boxed{6}\]

h.Generalize

Find the missing exponent: \((10^{4})^{2} = 10^{\square}\)

When a power is raised to a power, multiply the exponents:

\[(10^{4})^{2} = 10^{4 \cdot 2} = 10^{8}\]

\[\boxed{8}\]

i.Generalize

Find the missing exponent: \(n^{3} \cdot n^{4} = n^{\square}\)

The same rule works for any base. Add the exponents:

\[n^{3} \cdot n^{4} = n^{3+4} = n^{7}\]

\[\boxed{7}\]

Write in standard form: \(2\frac{1}{2}\) million

One million is \(1{,}000{,}000\). Two and a half million is:

\[2.5 \times 1{,}000{,}000 = 2{,}500{,}000\]

\[\boxed{2{,}500{,}000}\]

\[\boxed{15{,}000{,}000{,}000}\]

Problems 1–3 refer to the bar graph below.

1.

Refer to the graph to answer a and b. Answer true or false:

  1. a. Twice as many students walk to school as ride to school in a car.
  2. b. The majority of the students ride to school in either a bus or car.

First read the bar heights: Walk \(= 10\) students, Ride a Bike \(= 6\), Ride in a Car \(= 5\), Ride in a Bus \(= 12\). Total: \(10 + 6 + 5 + 12 = 33\) students.

a. Twice the number who ride in a car is \(2 \times 5 = 10\), and \(10\) students walk. The statement matches.

b. Bus or car: \(12 + 5 = 17\) students. Half of \(33\) is \(16.5\), and \(17 > 16.5\), so bus and car riders are more than half — a majority.

\[\boxed{\text{a. True} \qquad \text{b. True}}\]

Refer to the graph in problem 1. What is the ratio of those who walk to school to those who ride in a bus?

From the graph, \(10\) students walk and \(12\) ride in a bus:

\[\frac{10}{12} = \frac{5}{6}\]

\[\boxed{5:6}\]

\(12\) of the \(33\) students ride in a bus:

4.Explain

What is the mean (average) of these numbers? Describe how you found your answer and how you can check your work.

\[1.2,\quad 1.4,\quad 1.5,\quad 1.7,\quad 2\]

To find the mean, add all the numbers, then divide by how many numbers there are. First add:

\[1.2 + 1.4 + 1.5 + 1.7 + 2 = 7.8\]

There are \(5\) numbers, so divide:

\[7.8 \div 5 = 1.56\]

Check: multiply the mean by the number of values: \(1.56 \times 5 = 7.8\), which is the original sum, so the work checks.

\[\boxed{1.56}\]

Read this statement. Then answer the questions that follow.

One eighth of the \(40\) paintings were abstract.

  1. a. How many of the paintings were abstract?
  2. b. How many of the paintings were not abstract?

a. Take one eighth of \(40\):

\[\frac{1}{8} \times 40 = 5\]

b. Subtract from the total:

\[40 - 5 = 35\]

\[\boxed{\text{a. } 5 \text{ paintings} \qquad \text{b. } 35 \text{ paintings}}\]

7.

A gallon of water (\(128\) ounces) is poured into \(12\)-ounce glasses.

  1. a. How many glasses can be filled to the top?
  2. b. How many glasses are needed to hold all of the water?

Divide the total ounces by the size of each glass:

\[128 \div 12 = 10 \text{ R } 8\]

a. The quotient \(10\) is the number of glasses filled completely; \(8\) ounces are left over.

b. The leftover \(8\) ounces still need a glass, so one more glass is needed: \(10 + 1 = 11\).

\[\boxed{\text{a. } 10 \text{ glasses} \qquad \text{b. } 11 \text{ glasses}}\]

8.

A cubit is an ancient unit of measure equal to the distance from the elbow to the fingertips.

  1. a. Estimate the number of inches from your elbow to your fingertips.
  2. b. Measure the distance from your elbow to your fingertips to the nearest inch.

a. A cubit is roughly a foot and a half, so a good estimate is about \(18\) inches. Your own estimate may be a little more or less — that is fine.

b. There is no single printed answer for this part, because it depends on your own arm. To measure: hold a ruler (or tape measure) so one end is at the point of your elbow and read the mark at the tip of your middle finger, rounding to the nearest inch. Record the number you measure.

\[\boxed{\text{a. About } 18 \text{ in. (estimate)} \qquad \text{b. Your own measurement}}\]

9.
  1. a. Write \(0.375\) as a fraction.
  2. b. Write \(62\frac{1}{2}\%\) as a decimal.

14.Analyze

In figure \(ABCDEF\) all angles are right angles and \(AF = AB = BC\). Segment \(BC\) is twice the length of \(CD\). If \(\overline{CD}\) is \(3\) cm, what is the perimeter of the figure?

Start with \(CD = 3\) cm. Since \(BC\) is twice \(CD\):

\[BC = 2 \times 3 = 6 \text{ cm}\]

And \(AF = AB = BC\), so \(AF = AB = 6\) cm. Because all angles are right angles, the two unlabeled sides match combinations of the labeled ones:

\[DE = AF + BC = 6 + 6 = 12 \text{ cm}, \qquad EF = AB + CD = 6 + 3 = 9 \text{ cm}\]

Add all six sides:

\[6 + 6 + 6 + 3 + 12 + 9 = 42\]

\[\boxed{42 \text{ cm}}\]

27.

The figures below illustrate one triangle rotated into three different positions. Dimensions are in inches.

  1. a. What is the perimeter of the triangle?
  2. b. What is the area of the triangle?

It is the same triangle in all three positions. Each position rests on a different side, so the three bases — \(15\) in., \(13\) in., and \(14\) in. — are the three side lengths of the one triangle.

a. Perimeter is the sum of the three sides:

\[15 + 13 + 14 = 42 \text{ in.}\]

b. Use any base with its matching height, \(A = \frac{1}{2}bh\):

\[\tfrac{1}{2} \times 15 \times 11\tfrac{1}{5} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 15 \times \tfrac{56}{5} = 84 \text{ in.}^{2}\]

Check with the other pairs: \(\frac{1}{2} \times 13 \times 12\frac{12}{13} = 84\) and \(\frac{1}{2} \times 14 \times 12 = 84\). All three agree.

\[\boxed{\text{a. } 42 \text{ in.} \qquad \text{b. } 84 \text{ in.}^{2}}\]

28.Connect

Simplify and compare: \(125 \div 10^{2} \;\bigcirc\; 0.125 \times 10^{2}\)

Simplify each side. Dividing by \(10^{2}\) moves the decimal point \(2\) places left; multiplying by \(10^{2}\) moves it \(2\) places right:

\[125 \div 10^{2} = 1.25, \qquad 0.125 \times 10^{2} = 12.5\]

Since \(1.25 < 12.5\):

\[\boxed{125 \div 10^{2} < 0.125 \times 10^{2}}\]

30.

In this figure find the measure of

  1. a. \(\angle a\).
  2. b. \(\angle b\).
  3. c. Explain Describe how to find the measure of \(\angle c\).
  4. d. Name a supplementary angle pair in the figure.

a. \(\angle a\) sits on a straight line with the \(130^{\circ}\) angle, so they are supplementary:

\[\angle a = 180^{\circ} - 130^{\circ} = 50^{\circ}\]

b. The three interior angles of a triangle add to \(180^{\circ}\):

\[\angle b = 180^{\circ} - 65^{\circ} - 50^{\circ} = 65^{\circ}\]

c. \(\angle c\) is an exterior angle at the bottom-right vertex. It forms a linear pair with \(\angle b\) on the straight base line, so it is supplementary to \(\angle b\):

\[\angle c = 180^{\circ} - \angle b = 180^{\circ} - 65^{\circ} = 115^{\circ}\]

(Equivalently, by the exterior angle theorem, \(\angle c\) equals the sum of the two remote interior angles: \(65^{\circ} + 50^{\circ} = 115^{\circ}\).)

d. \(\angle a\) and the \(130^{\circ}\) angle form a linear pair, so they are supplementary. (Another pair: \(\angle b\) and \(\angle c\).)

\[\boxed{\angle a = 50^{\circ} \qquad \angle b = 65^{\circ} \qquad \angle c = 115^{\circ}}\]
