If the product of two one-digit whole numbers is 45, what is the sum of the same two numbers?

Solution:

The single-digit whole numbers that can multiply to give 45 are $5$ and $9$ ($5 \times 9 = 45$).

Their sum is: $5 + 9 = \mathbf{14}$

6.

List the single-digit factors of 756.

Solution:

Evaluating divisibility rules for numbers 1 through 9:

  • 1: Every integer is divisible by 1 $\rightarrow$ Yes
  • 2: Ends in an even digit (6) $\rightarrow$ Yes
  • 3: Sum of digits ($7+5+6=18$) is divisible by 3 $\rightarrow$ Yes
  • 4: Last two digits (56) are divisible by 4 ($56 \div 4 = 14$) $\rightarrow$ Yes
  • 5: Does not end in 0 or 5 $\rightarrow$ No
  • 6: Completely divisible by both 2 and 3 $\rightarrow$ Yes
  • 7: $756 \div 7 = 108$ exactly $\rightarrow$ Yes
  • 8: Last three digits ($756 \div 8 = 94.5$) leave a remainder $\rightarrow$ No
  • 9: Sum of digits (18) is divisible by 9 $\rightarrow$ Yes

The single-digit factors are: $\mathbf{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 9}$

The outside temperature at 6:00 a.m. was $-6^\circ\text{C}$. By 3:00 p.m., the temperature had risen to $5^\circ\text{C}$. Did the temperature rise or fall between dawn and mid-afternoon? By how many degrees?

Solution:

Because the value moved from negative values to a higher positive integer, the temperature rose.

Finding the change: $$5^\circ\text{C} - (-6^\circ\text{C}) = 5 + 6 = 11^\circ\text{C}$$

The temperature rose by $11^\circ\text{C}$.

No, it does not leave a remainder.

JUSTIFY

Show the factoring steps and algebraic properties that make this product quick to solve mentally: $5 \cdot 13 \cdot 20$

Solution:

$$5 \cdot 13 \cdot 20 = 5 \cdot 20 \cdot 13 \quad \text{(Commutative Property of Multiplication)}$$

$$= (5 \cdot 20) \cdot 13 \quad \text{(Associative Property of Multiplication)}$$

$$= 100 \cdot 13 = \mathbf{1300}$$

24.

Compare: $8000 \div (400 \div 20) \bigcirc (8000 \div 400) \div 20$

Solution:

Left side evaluation: $8000 \div 20 = 400$

Right side evaluation: $20 \div 20 = 1$

Since $400 > 1$:

$$8000 \div (400 \div 20) \mathbf{\Huge >} (8000 \div 400) \div 20$$

CONCLUDE

If you were given the total measure of segment $PR$ ($m\overline{PR}$) and the specific segment measure of $QR$ ($m\overline{QR}$), explain how you would calculate the unknown segment length of $PQ$ ($m\overline{PQ}$).

Answer:

You would subtract the length of segment $QR$ from the entire system length $PR$.

$$m\overline{PQ} = m\overline{PR} - m\overline{QR}$$
